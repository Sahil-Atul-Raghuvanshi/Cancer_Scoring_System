What this screen is for

Everywhere else in this project, a model is checked against another model. This screen is the exception. BRACS published a whole slide together with boxes a pathologist drew round each lesion, saying which are invasive cancer and which are still inside a duct. Neither model has seen this slide.

So for the first time there is a right answer to compare against. Four pictures of the same tissue: what it looks like, what the pathologist said, what our model said, and what BEETLE said.

Only the tissue inside a box is scored. BRACS drew boxes round lesions only, so a perfectly ordinary duct sitting outside every box is real epithelium that simply was not annotated — marking it wrong would punish both models for finding something that is genuinely there.

The technical version

The whole tissue is segmented at 0.5 µm/px — 17×18 mm, 24,021 tiles of 224 px. It is walked in blocks with a 256 px halo that is segmented and then thrown away, because a U-Net is worst at the edge of its receptive field and a block segmented to its own border leaves a seam across every duct on the boundary. Only three things are kept: one label per tile from each model plus the pathologist's, which is a 157×153 grid rather than 1.8 gigapixels.

The annotations are QuPath .qpdata — Java-serialised, normally readable only by QuPath. bracs_app/qpdata.py reads the rectangles directly, checking the file's own field descriptor before trusting a single coordinate, and refuses outright if it finds polygons rather than reading a partial ground truth. An empty ground truth would score every model as perfect.

Label mapping. Malignant-sure → invasive, DCIS-sure → in-situ, and UDH-sure → in-situ as well: usual ductal hyperplasia is a benign proliferation inside a duct, which is non-invasive epithelium by the same definition that puts DCIS there. Anything BRACS marked uncertain is left unlabelled rather than scored against a maybe.

What this screen is for

Two models can now colour tissue: the one we trained, and BEETLE, the ready-made one that produced its training examples. BEETLE is about thirty times slower and its licence forbids commercial use, so one question is worth answering before anything else: do we still need it?

Below, both are given exactly the same piece of tissue from each of our six patients — same place, same magnification, same three colours. Your eye is the instrument: do the two right-hand pictures agree about where the ducts are?

One warning before you read anything into agreement. Our model learned from BEETLE, so the two agreeing mostly shows the copying worked — not that either is right. The informative part is where they disagree, and that neither model has ever seen tissue from this laboratory.

The technical version

One 2048 px window per slide at 0.5 µm/px — 1.02 mm of tissue. Both models must run at that spacing (BEETLE's dataset.json says "spacing": 0.5; the checkpoint's manifest says mpp: 0.5), and a whole slide at it is ~400 MPx, roughly an hour per fold. A lower-magnification overview would put both models outside their trained scale and measure that instead of them.

The window is the most nucleus-dense one on the slide, scored by beetle_teacher_resnet18/src/regions.py at 2 µm/px over step 3's tissue mask. Nuclear density is a stain measurement rather than a model output, so it favours neither model, and epithelium is nuclear-dense — which keeps the comparison off a blank field of stroma that both models would agree about for free.

BEETLE labels every pixel; our model labels a 224 px tile, which is why its column is blocky. That blockiness is its real resolution and is deliberately not smoothed. Agreement is scored per tile against BEETLE's majority label in the same tile — scoring per pixel would charge our model for its output format rather than its judgement. The per-pixel figure is shown too, as the pessimistic one.

What this screen is for

This project's own experiment runs on a fixed collection of 665 pictures, from the command line. This screen runs the same model on any picture you give it, so you can see for yourself what it does and where it goes wrong.

Drop in a photograph of stained breast tissue — the ordinary pink-and-purple kind, H&E — and the model will colour every pixel of it as cancer inside a duct, cancer that has broken out, or everything else.

Nothing you upload here becomes training data, and nothing you upload is counted in any measurement this project reports. It is coloured, shown to you, and left in a folder of its own.

The technical version, and the two ways this misleads you

The image is resampled once to 0.5 µm/px with area averaging and passed through BEETLE's released nnU-Net exactly as a BRACS region is — same weights, same half-step sliding window, same Gaussian blending, same 5-class argmax translated into BCSS codes by labels.to_bcss_codes. The uint16 mask written here is byte-for-byte the same kind of file as the ones the batch pipeline writes. What is not done is the tile export: export_tiles=False, so no upload can reach data/runs/, the stain comparison, or approach 1's training set.

1. The resolution field is not decoration. It sets the physical size of what the model looks at. Tell it 0.25 µm/px when the image is really 0.5 and every nucleus arrives twice its trained size; the model will not error, it will return a confident, wrong picture. If you do not know the number, note that 40× is usually 0.25 and 20× usually 0.5.

2. It will answer confidently about tissue it has never seen. BEETLE was trained on H&E breast sections. Given an immunohistochemistry slide, another organ, a frozen section, or a photograph of a screen, it still returns a full three-colour mask, because an argmax over five channels always names a winner. There is no "I don't know" output. The model's confidence figure below measures how peaked the softmax was, which is not the same thing as being right.
